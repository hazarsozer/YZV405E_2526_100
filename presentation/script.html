Part 1.Hazar — Setup & Architecture
~ 2:00 · slides 1–3
01Title
0:15

Hi everyone. We're Group 100 — I'm Hazar, that's Faruk, and that's Enis. Our project is multimodal idiom ranking — from the AdMIRe 2.0 task at the MWE workshop, EACL 2026.

02Task
0:45

So the task is this: given a sentence containing an idiom and five candidate images, we have to rank the images by how well they match the meaning the sentence intends.

Here's a quick example. Take the phrase "bad apple". Literally, that's a rotten piece of fruit. But figuratively, it's one bad person whose behavior spreads to others. Same two words, completely different meaning — and only the sentence around it tells you which one is meant. PAUSE

The dataset has fifteen languages and around twenty-six hundred sentences. But here's the catch — only English and Brazilian Portuguese have labeled training data. The other thirteen languages are completely zero-shot, we never see a labeled example. The competition metric is Top-1 accuracy, averaged across all fifteen languages.

03Pipeline
1:00

Here's how our pipeline works. Four phases, all running sequentially on a single GPU, peak memory just 3.5 gigabytes.

Phase A is text prep. The sentence gets translated to English by NLLB-200. Then Phi-3.5-mini rewrites that English sentence, replacing the idiom with its plain-language meaning. We call that rewrite the paraphrase.

Phase B is encoding. BGE-M3 embeds three things — the English sentence, the paraphrase, and the five image captions.

Phase C is the sense decision. A calibrated logistic regression — ninety-four percent accurate on the English dev set — outputs the probability that the sentence is idiomatic. A gate uses that probability to pick either the paraphrase or the original sentence as the final query.

Phase D is the ranking. Cosine similarity between the chosen query and each caption. Highest score wins. PAUSE

One important point: every encoder in this pipeline is frozen. The only trained piece in the whole system is that small LR head.

HAZAR → FARUK "Now Faruk will walk us through what we actually found."
Part 2.Faruk — Findings & Results
~ 2:00 · slides 4–5
04Discovery
0:55

The biggest single finding in the whole project came from one substitution.

Our original proposal used SigLIP2 — a vision-language model that aligns images and text directly. With that, we scored 0.30.

We replaced SigLIP2 with BGE-M3 doing pure text matching against the English image captions. The score jumped to 0.37. That's a twenty-three percent relative improvement, just from one design change. PAUSE

Two reasons this worked. First, SigLIP2's text encoder is mostly trained on English image-text pairs, so it gets weak on translated, idiomatic input. Second, the image captions are always in English — that gives us a clean, single-language channel that BGE-M3 was actually built for.

And here's the interesting part: when we added SigLIP2 image features back on top of the BGE caption matching, it didn't help at all. The text channel alone was enough.

05Results
1:05

This is the full picture. Every strategy we tried, ranked from worst to best.

Vanilla SigLIP2 with no classifier scored 0.28. Our original proposed pipeline scored 0.30. Then BGE captions started entering the picture. Always-paraphrase BGE gave us 0.35. A cross-encoder reranker — which usually beats bi-encoders on retrieval — actually hurt here, also 0.35.

A three-channel blend of original captions, enriched captions, and SigLIP2 image features got us to 0.36. PAUSE

Two strategies tied at 0.37. One was a SigLIP plus BGE ensemble — adding the vision channel back. The other was our chosen winner: BGE captions with the classifier-gated query selection. Since they tied, we picked the simpler one. Occam's razor.

For reference, on the previous version of this task — AdMIRe 1.0 — human raters scored 0.83, and the top SemEval team scored 0.93. So we're well below human performance. Enis will explain why in a moment.

Part 3.Enis — Ablations & Diagnosis
~ 2:00 · slides 6–7
06Ablations
0:55

We ran six rounds of ablation. Every single round was an attempt to push past 0.37 — and every single one failed.

Round one was the backbone switch Faruk just described — that's the only one that actually moved the score.

In round two, we tried a bigger paraphraser, Qwen-7B instead of Phi-3.5. Same exact score, 0.37. So paraphrase quality is not the bottleneck.

In round three, we replaced the bi-encoder with a cross-encoder reranker. That actually hurt by 0.02. We took it as diagnostic — this task isn't standard retrieval.

Round four fixed a Portuguese routing bug and tried soft blending the literal and figurative scores. No measurable gain. Round five was a threshold sweep — completely flat at 0.37 for any threshold above 0.60.

Round six, we enriched the captions with extra idiom-aware context, generated by Phi-3.5. That hurt by 0.04. PAUSE

So 0.37 just wouldn't move. We needed a different explanation for why.

07Diagnosis
1:00

When we wrote our report, we attributed the 0.37 ceiling to one cause — what we called the visual metaphor gap. Captions describe what an image literally shows — a rotten apple on a table. Idioms describe what it means — one bad influence corrupting others. No text-to-text model can bridge that gap.

But last night, we actually opened up NLLB's translation output for the first time and read what it had been producing. And we found a second cause we hadn't tested.

For example, in Turkish, the idiom "çürük elma" — which literally means rotten apple — gets translated by NLLB to "rotten elmas". The word "elma" stays in Turkish. The system half-translates the idiom and gives up. PAUSE We saw the same pattern in Russian, Chinese, and Spanish.

So the 0.37 ceiling actually has two compounding causes: translation, and modality. Future work splits cleanly — a stronger machine translation model for the upstream gap, and vision-language models with metaphor priors, like GPT-4V or LLaVA-Next, for the downstream one.

Code and full ablations are on our repo. Happy to take questions.

Q&A.All three — anticipated questions
~ 2:00

Why didn't you fine-tune anything?

Two reasons. First, we had a hard 12-gigabyte VRAM budget, and sequential model loading already gets us to 3.5 gigabytes peak — fine-tuning BGE-M3 needs much more memory than that. Second, only English and Brazilian Portuguese have labeled training data, so we couldn't meaningfully fine-tune for the other thirteen languages anyway. Keeping everything frozen means the same pipeline serves all fifteen languages without retraining.

Why did SigLIP2 do worse than text-only BGE?

SigLIP2's text encoder is trained primarily on English image-text pairs from the web. When you feed it translated, idiomatic input, it underperforms a dedicated multilingual encoder like BGE-M3. The captions, on the other hand, are always in English — they give BGE-M3 a clean, single-language signal it was actually designed for.

Did you check that translation actually preserves the idiom?

Yes — and that's exactly what slide 7 is about. We audited NLLB's output after submission, and we found it frequently corrupts idiomatic content. Turkish was the worst case — sometimes the compound is left untranslated entirely, sometimes the surrounding context gets hallucinated. That's the second source of the ceiling we mentioned.

Why 0.37 and not closer to the 0.83 human score?

Two compounding gaps. Translation loses signal upstream — sometimes catastrophically. The modality gap loses signal downstream. Even if we had a perfect translator, we'd still hit the modality wall — and even with perfect modality reasoning, NLLB's broken translations would limit us. The two problems stack.

Did you try retrieval over a symbol bank or external knowledge?

Not directly. Round six was the closest — we added an LLM-generated symbolic sentence to each caption to give it more abstract context. That actually hurt the score by 0.04. We think the right move is cross-modal reasoning over the image itself, not adding more text — which is why we're pointing toward vision-language models for future work.

Why is the cross-encoder worse than the bi-encoder?

Cross-encoders are great when the relevance task is over semantically similar text. Here, the figurative caption describes a scene that depicts an abstract concept — there's a literal-vs-figurative gap. The cross-encoder sees that semantic gap and scores it low. The bi-encoder cosine is more forgiving, and that's actually helpful here.

What's the runtime?

After translations and embeddings are cached — that's a one-time cost of a few hours — re-ranking the full fifteen-language test set takes just a few minutes. Caching is what makes the ablation work feasible.
